# Wording

How NeonRadar words what it says about people, companies and accounts: it describes patterns and never makes accusations (guardrail G5 in [docs/build/PHASE-1.md](build/PHASE-1.md) section 2.1). This page holds the disclaimers, the vocabulary for entity-facing text, the forbidden patterns and the documentation rules, with the reasons for each. The source of truth is PHASE-1.md section 7.1; terms are defined in the [glossary](glossary.md).

There are two kinds of text, with different rules:

- **Entity-facing text** is everything NeonRadar shows next to a specific entity: alert headlines, summaries and steps, interface labels and, from Phase 4, the copilot's answers. It uses the vocabulary below and must pass the wording lint.
- **Documentation** (typology explainers, the glossary, case notes, these pages) discusses money laundering as a subject. It may use domain terms such as "shell company chains" because they describe a pattern, not an entity, and it follows the documentation rules below.

## Disclaimers

The four texts below (three disclaimers and the README limitations text) are verbatim. Milestone M1.7c commits the disclaimers as text files in `contracts/wording/`, and `GET /api/v1/meta` serves them; this page and those files must stay identical. Since C3 1.1.0 (PHASE-2.md 5.3) the real data notice is version 2; version 1 (`real-data-v1.txt`) stays in the folder unchanged, for the record.

**Standing disclaimer** (`contracts/wording/standing-v1.txt`). Shown near the top of the README, on every interface page that shows entities or alerts, and served by the API.

```text
NeonRadar is an educational, open-source tool that finds structural patterns in synthetic or public data. A pattern match is not evidence of money laundering, corruption or any other wrongdoing. There are legitimate reasons for almost every structure shown here, including holding companies, shared registered agents, professional directors, payment intermediaries and cash businesses. Where public data is used, many people and entities share the same or similar names, and source registers contain errors. Nothing here suggests or implies that any identifiable person or entity has broken the law. NeonRadar is not a certified compliance system and does not make anyone compliant with anything. If you find an error in NeonRadar, tell us and we will correct it; errors in source data can only be corrected by the source, and we will tell you where to report them.
```

**Synthetic demo notice** (`contracts/wording/synthetic-demo-v1.txt`). Shown when every loaded dataset is synthetic, and in every screenshot and recording of the demo.

```text
Synthetic data. Every company, person, address, account and payment in this demonstration was generated by NeonRadar, and none of them exists. Patterns that resemble money laundering were planted on purpose to show how such structures look in a graph, next to ordinary activity that only looks similar. Detection results on synthetic data say nothing about how NeonRadar would perform on real data.
```

**Real data notice** (`contracts/wording/real-data-v2.txt`, verbatim from PHASE-2.md 5.3). Shown whenever any loaded dataset has `synthetic: false`.

```text
This view includes records from public sources (company registers, the GLEIF index, the ICIJ Offshore Leaks Database, which is built from leaked files, official sanctions lists and, where chosen, PEP data), listed with their licences on the Data sources page. Records about real people and companies are shown as their source published them and may be out of date. Where several records appear to describe the same person or company, NeonRadar may show them as one entity; a link marked as a possible match is not confirmed. NeonRadar adds no allegations: any payment activity shown next to a real entity is synthetic background activity, and a pattern that touches a real entity is a structural observation only. Names can match the wrong person or company, so confirm identities with addresses, dates or official identifiers before drawing any conclusion. A missing sanctions or PEP flag means only that the lists loaded here do not name the record. These records can include personal data, and whoever loads them is responsible for handling it lawfully.
```

**README limitations text.** The "Limitations" section of the README, verbatim. It carries the three statements the project plan requires: synthetic transactions are not real laundering, detection rates on synthetic data say nothing about real data, and NeonRadar is not a certified compliance system.

```text
Synthetic transactions are not real money laundering: every planted scenario is invented, and so is every payment in the demo. Detection rates measured on synthetic data say nothing about how NeonRadar would perform on real data. NeonRadar is not a certified compliance system and makes no one compliant with any law or regulation.
```

### Why the disclaimers say this

They follow the pattern that public data tools use: legitimate uses exist, being included or matching a pattern does not imply wrongdoing, names and records can be wrong, and corrections are welcome.

- ICIJ's Offshore Leaks Database says that "there are legitimate uses for offshore companies and trusts", that inclusion "is not intended to suggest or imply that they have engaged in illegal or improper conduct", and that "many people and entities have the same or similar names", so identities should be confirmed with addresses or other identifiable information [icij_faq].
- OpenSanctions says that its label "entity of interest" "does not carry a specific legal meaning, and does not imply wrongdoing" [opensanctions_glossary].
- OpenCorporates warns that source data can contain "errors which we have not yet detected" [opencorporates_terms], and the former Open Ownership Register warned that its original sources may contain errors and welcomed error reports [openownership_register_terms].

NeonRadar adds what is specific to it: it is educational, it finds structural patterns, it is not a compliance system, and errors in source data can be corrected only at the source.

## Vocabulary for entity-facing text

| Write | Never write | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "structural pattern present", "matches the graph pattern of <typology name>" | "money laundering", "suspicious activity" | A pattern match is not evidence of money laundering. Suspicion is a judgement a person makes with the full facts, and for a regulated institution it can trigger a report to the financial intelligence unit [fatf_recommendations_2026]; a graph query cannot make that judgement. |
| "funds returned to the originating account", "transfers", "payments" | "laundered funds", "dirty money" | The data shows how money moved, not where it came from. |
| "listed in <dataset> as sanctioned", "as a politically exposed person", "as a relative or close associate of a politically exposed person" | "sanctioned criminal", "corrupt official" | Flags come from source data and say only what the source published. No other source topics are shown next to entities. |
| "shares a registered address with 14 other companies" | "shell company", "front company" (about a specific entity) | Describe the structure, not a label. Most shell companies are formed for legitimate purposes [fincen_fin_2006_g014], and the glossary terms describe structures, not a verdict on a named company. |
| "pattern strength" | "risk score", "suspicion score", "likelihood of laundering" | Pattern strength measures how closely the evidence matches a typology's graph pattern (C4 alert contract). It is not a probability of anything. |
| "synthetic scenario" (only when the alert's `wording_class` is `synthetic_scenario`: every evidence node, subject and evidence-account holder is synthetic) | "laundering case", "the launderer" | Only scenarios the generator planted among synthetic entities are scenarios at all; nothing NeonRadar shows next to an entity is a case about a real person. |
| "linked to", "holds an account that received" | "accomplice", "mule", "fraudster" | These words claim knowledge or intent. Money mules can act "wittingly or unwittingly" [fincen_fin_2025_a003], and the graph shows neither. |

The display name of any typology that has a detector must pass the lint, because alert and interface text say "matches the graph pattern of <name>".

The lint (`scripts/check_wording.py`, run by `make hygiene` and CI) applies to NeonRadar's own entity-facing wording: typology `wording` templates, string literals in `detect/src/**/wording.py` and (from Phase 2) `graph/src/**/wording.py`, rendered alert text (headline, summary and steps, with entity captions and account numbers masked out) and, from Phase 4, the copilot's answer templates. It does not apply to the disclaimers, typology names and explainers, the glossary, case notes or other documentation, which discuss money laundering as a subject. Phase 5 adds Arabic equivalents when the interface is translated.

## Documentation rules

Rules 1 to 3 are also in `.claude/rules/aml-docs.md`, for AI sessions; rules 4 and 5 are only here.

1. Every factual statement about money laundering cites a key from the [bibliography](sources/bibliography.yaml) ([how to cite](sources/README.md)). Quote sources in quotation marks with the key; otherwise paraphrase and cite.
2. Use the terms in the [glossary](glossary.md). "Beneficial owner" means a natural person only.
3. Real cases name as wrongdoers only institutions that admitted facts and individuals who were convicted, with the court level. Victims, publishers, courts and authorities may be named in those roles. Everyone else is described by role. Separate what was adjudicated from what is alleged, and give the date the status was checked.
4. Never call a real, named company a shell, a front or a mule account. Describe the structure instead.
5. A red flag or indicator is a reason to look closer, not evidence. The FFIEC says its red flags "are not all-inclusive" and warns that a red flag alone does not prove criminal activity [ffiec_appendix_f].

## The scale of money laundering

Use this statement in the README, slides and anywhere else that gives a global figure:

> UNODC estimated that about USD 1.6 trillion, or 2.7 percent of global GDP, was laundered in 2009, and attributes the often-quoted range of 2 to 5 percent to the IMF in 1998 [unodc_iff_2011].

Do not write that UNODC estimates "2 to 5 percent" or "as much as 5 percent of GDP": that range is the IMF's 1998 figure, which UNODC cites [unodc_iff_2011] (PHASE-1.md section 0.5, item 9).
